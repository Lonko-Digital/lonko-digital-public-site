Google Ads Getting Clicks But No Leads? What to Check First

Pillar: Understanding Your Numbers·10 min read

In short

If your Google Ads are getting clicks but no leads, the cause is usually one of eight things, and they are worth checking in a particular order. Start by confirming the leads are really missing, then check whether the campaign is measuring them, then work forward through who is clicking, where they land, and what happens after they get in touch. Only after that does it make sense to ask whether more budget would help.

The order matters because the early checks can change the meaning of everything after them. If tracking is broken, the search terms and landing page may be fine. If the leads are arriving but nobody is answering them, no ad change will fix it.

This is one of the most common things a business owner notices and can't explain. You can see the clicks and the spend. You can't see why nothing is coming back. Here is how to work through it calmly.

A note on scope: this checklist applies most directly to Google Search campaigns. Performance Max, Demand Gen and other campaign types offer different controls and reports, so some steps, especially the ones about search terms and keyword settings, work differently there.

The short version

  1. Confirm the leads are actually missing.
  2. Check that conversion tracking is measuring what you think it is.
  3. Read the search terms that triggered your ads.
  4. Check who you are reaching: targeting and location.
  5. Check that the ad and the landing page say the same thing.
  6. Look at the landing page itself: friction and offer.
  7. Follow a real enquiry after it arrives.
  8. Decide whether more budget is the answer.

You do not need to be an expert to do the first few. Most of them take a short look, not a rebuild.

If you want to skip ahead, this table matches what you are seeing to the most likely break and the first thing to check.

What you are seeing Likely break First thing to check

Very few clicks so far, and no leads Too little data The number of clicks and the conversion window (step 1)
The search terms are mostly irrelevant Traffic and search intent The search terms report and your negative keywords (step 3)
Clicks come from the wrong places or at the wrong hours Targeting Location settings and when the clicks arrive (step 4)
The search terms look relevant, but few people get in touch Landing page, offer or friction Whether the page matches the ad, and how it works on a phone (steps 5 and 6)

1. Confirm the leads are actually missing

Before changing anything, check whether leads are missing or only unrecorded. Those are different problems with different fixes.

Look outside the ad platform. Check your inbox, your phone log, your form notifications, your booking system and your spam folder. Ask whoever answers the phone whether calls have changed. If enquiries are arriving but Google Ads shows none, the campaign may be fine and the measurement is the problem. If nothing is arriving anywhere, the problem is real and the next steps apply.

Also check the time window. A lead that arrives days after a click can still be counted back to that click, as long as it falls inside the conversion window. For Search and Display click-through conversions, the default click-through conversion window is 30 days unless it has been changed. Recent days can look emptier than they are because late leads have not come in yet.

Finally, count the clicks. With a small number of clicks, zero leads can be ordinary chance. As an illustration only, not a benchmark: if one click in twenty normally becomes a lead, twenty clicks will produce none about a third of the time. A short, quiet stretch is not always a problem. If volume is low, give it more time before you change things.

2. Check that conversion tracking is measuring what you think it is

If the platform cannot see a lead, it will report none, however many you receive. This is a common reason a campaign looks like it is failing when it is not.

In Google Ads, the list of conversion actions shows a status for each one. At the time of writing it sits under Goals, then Summary, and Google describes statuses such as Active, Needs attention, Misconfigured and Awaiting conversions (no conversions recorded in the last 7 days). A status of Active is a good sign. It is not proof that the right thing is being counted.

So test it yourself. Submit your own form, or call the number your ad or page uses, and check that it appears. Then confirm the action being counted is a real lead. A common problem is counting something easy, such as a page view or a button click, so the numbers look healthy while real enquiries go unmeasured. The opposite also happens: a form that works, but a thank-you page that was changed during a site update, so the tag no longer fires.

If phone calls are a main source of leads, check that calls are being counted too. And if your leads go into a CRM or a spreadsheet, compare that count with what the platform reports. A gap between the two is a finding, not an inconvenience.

For more on why this matters, see The Real Cost of Not Tracking Conversions.

3. Read the search terms

The search terms report shows the actual searches that triggered your ads. It is often the most revealing page in the account, because it shows what people typed, not what you hoped they would type.

Look for searches that are not from someone who could become a customer: people looking for a job, a free option, a definition, a do-it-yourself guide, or a service you do not offer. If a large share of your spend is going to searches like these, you are paying for clicks that were never likely to become leads. Irrelevant terms can usually be blocked with negative keywords. Broader match behavior can expose ads to a wider range of searches, which makes reviewing the search terms report especially important.

Two cautions. Google does not show every search. Terms with too little activity are left out of the report to protect privacy, so the report is a good sample, not a full list. And a search term that has not converted yet is not automatically a bad one. Judge terms by what the searcher probably wanted, not only by this month's count.

4. Check who you are reaching

Even relevant searches can come from the wrong people. Two common causes are location and timing.

For location, check which areas the campaign targets and which it excludes. If you serve one city, a region that is much too wide sends clicks from people you cannot serve. Then check the location option. Google's default, "Presence or interest", reaches people who are in or regularly in your area and also people who have shown interest in it. The narrower option, "Presence", reaches only people likely to be in the area. For a business that can only serve people in one place, it is worth deciding on purpose which of the two applies, instead of leaving the default in place unexamined.

Then look at the rest of the picture: the devices, the times of day, and the days of the week with the most clicks. If most of your clicks arrive at hours when no one can answer, or from a device where the page works badly, that shows up here.

5. Check that the ad and the landing page say the same thing

Someone clicks because the ad made a promise. If the page does not repeat that promise within a few seconds, they leave.

Read your top ad, then open the page it sends people to, as if you had never seen either. Does the page answer the same question the ad raised? If the ad offers a specific service, an area or a price, the first screen should say the same thing. An ad for one service that lands on a general homepage forces the visitor to work out where to go. Many will not.

If the account sends many different ads to one general page, that is worth fixing before increasing budget or making broader campaign changes. A page built around one clear request usually serves a visitor better than a page built around everything the business does.

6. Look at the page itself

Once the message matches, look at what the page asks of a visitor. Open it on a phone, because that is where much of the traffic arrives.

Ask a few plain questions. Does it load quickly enough? Is it obvious what to do next? Is the phone number tappable? Is the form short enough that a busy person would finish it? Does the page explain what happens after they get in touch? And is there a real reason to act, such as a clear offer, a specific next step, or a reason to trust you now?

A page can be well designed and still weak on the offer. If a visitor cannot tell what they would get, how much effort it takes, or why you rather than the next result, clicks tend to stay clicks.

7. Follow a real enquiry after it arrives

This step is easy to skip, and it is where otherwise sound campaigns can quietly lose leads.

Send yourself an enquiry, or ask a colleague to, and follow it. Where does it land? Who sees it? How long does it take for someone to respond? Does it land in a spam folder, an inbox no one checks, or a form tool that never forwards it? Are calls going to voicemail at the busiest times?

Then look at the leads you do receive. If many are people you cannot help, the issue may be what the ad and page attract, or what the business is asking for. If they are the right people but few turn into customers, the cause may be in the follow-up rather than in the ads. Either way, the campaign cannot fix it, and it is much better to know.

8. Decide whether more budget is the answer

When results disappoint, raising the budget is a natural instinct. It is also the check to leave for last.

More budget extends what the campaign is already doing. It does not repair a measurement gap, a mismatch between ad and page, or a slow response to enquiries. If the first seven steps are unresolved, more spend usually means more of the same result, only larger.

Two questions help. First, can you currently tell a lead from a non-lead, reliably, in the platform? If not, more budget adds cost without adding clarity. Second, has the result held across several periods, rather than relying on one good or bad stretch? If you are not sure how to read a short run of numbers, see Why One Good Week Doesn't Mean It's Working.

If the early steps are sound and results are steady, a budget increase becomes a reasonable, measured decision. If they are not, fixing those first is almost always the better use of the next dollar.

What to do this week

You do not need to do everything at once. A sensible order for the next few days:

  • Check your inbox, phone log and spam folder against what the platform reports.
  • Submit a test enquiry and confirm it appears as a conversion.
  • Open the search terms report and read the top terms by spend.
  • Open your landing page on a phone and ask whether it matches the ad.
  • Follow one test enquiry all the way to a reply.
