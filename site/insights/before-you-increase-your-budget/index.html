Before You Increase Your Marketing Budget

Pillar: How to Think About a Decision·October 13, 2026·3 min read

In short

More budget buys more of what you are already getting, not necessarily more of what you want. Before increasing spend, check that the current result is real, that there is room to grow, that the next dollar will perform like the average one, and how you will know if it worked.

Someone suggests spending more. Maybe it's your agency. Maybe it's a platform nudging you with a recommendation. Maybe it's your own instinct after a good month. The logic sounds simple: if it's working, put more into it.

Sometimes that's exactly right. But more budget only buys more of what you're already getting if a few things are true. Four questions help you check before you commit.

Is the result you're seeing real?

Increasing spend is a bet that your current results will continue. If those results came from one unusually good stretch, you'd be scaling something that may not repeat.

Before you add budget, make sure the result has held across multiple periods rather than relying on one unusually good stretch, and that you have a reasonable idea of what's behind it. A result you can explain is a safer thing to scale than one you can't.

Is there room to grow?

Every campaign has a ceiling. Only so many people in your area search for what you sell, or fit your audience, in any given week. If you're already reaching most of them, extra budget has nowhere useful to go.

Start with whether the campaign is consistently able to use its current budget. If it regularly leaves budget unused, the constraint is probably somewhere else, and raising the budget alone is unlikely to help. If it consistently uses the available budget while worthwhile demand remains, there may be room to grow.

The average dollar tells you what happened. The next dollar is the one you're deciding about.

Will the next dollar perform like the average dollar?

Your results so far are an average. Say you've been getting leads at about $50 each. That's the average across everything you've spent. It doesn't promise the next $500 will cost $50 per lead.

Often, the first dollars reach the best opportunities, the people most likely to respond. Later dollars reach people who are a little less likely to. So the cost of each additional result tends to rise as spend rises.

That changes the question. Instead of asking what your average cost per lead is, ask what a lead is worth to you at the cost you'd actually be paying for the extra ones.

How will you know it worked?

Before you change anything, decide three things. How much will you add? How long will you give it? What result would count as success?

Write those down, and decide in advance what you'll do if it doesn't work. Scale back? Hold steady? Having that answer ready makes it much easier to act on the evidence instead of arguing with it after the money is spent.

It also helps to increase in steps rather than all at once. A smaller increase is easier to reverse and gives you a read before you've committed to much.

The short version

A budget increase is a decision about the next dollar, not the average one. Check that the result is real, that there's room to grow, that the extra spend will still pay off, and how you'll tell.
